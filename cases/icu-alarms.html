Case study · Interface ergonomics

When several alarms go off, is the most urgent one still the most visible?

When an operator overlooks an alarm, we call it human error. In human factors, we call it a design error: the person did not fail, the system was designed without regard for human limits.

  • Cognitive ergonomics
  • Visual attention modelling
  • IEC 62366-1
  • IEC 60073

Study conducted by Esteffe Violet

Published 16 November 2026

Full report available on request

Context

Hospitals, where alarms are everywhere

According to a review of 72 publications, 80 to 99% of the alarms raised by medical devices in hospitals are false or clinically insignificant [1]. Over time, staff get used to them and the risk of ignoring one grows: this is known as alarm fatigue.

The usual response is to reduce the number of alarms. We looked at something a manufacturer controls directly: their visibility. When several alarms are displayed at once, is the most serious one still the most visible to the operator?

The problem

The more alarms there are, the less you see the most serious one

With a single alarm, all is well. The life-threatening alarm is the most visible.

With four more alarms, it loses 41% of its visibility. It has not changed, yet it becomes less visible than the other four.

In practice: when several alarms trigger at once, the interface does not steer attention to the most urgent one. On the contrary, the screen draws the eye first to a secondary, non-urgent alarm, and only then to the life-threatening one. In real conditions, this can lead to a life-threatening emergency being overlooked or treated late.

The cause

Three design choices behind the problem

  1. Red is the darkest colourOn a dark background, yellow and cyan stand out more. The colour that means “most serious” is the one that stands out least.
  2. All alarms share the same formatSame size, same position, same typography. Severity is coded by colour alone.
  3. The screen does not adaptOne alarm or five, the display is the same. Nothing does the sorting for the clinician.

Research on visual perception explains the result: a unique item is spotted at a glance, but as soon as several look alike, they have to be checked one by one [2].

Our method

An audit grounded in standards and a visual attention model

  1. FrameFour conditions, with a growing number of alarms on screen.
  2. Measure44 ergonomic criteria, and the visibility of each alarm quantified by a model.
  3. AnalyseResult cross-checked with two other models.
  4. RecommendRecommendations, interface redesign, then a before/after comparison with the same measure.

The interface was assessed against established ergonomic criteria [3, 4] and checked against two international standards: IEC 62366-1, which governs the usability engineering of medical devices, and IEC 60073, which sets the coding rules for colours and signals [5, 6].

The visibility of each alarm is estimated by our visual attention model. It combines two published methods: the spectral residual, which picks up what stands out by its structure (a sharp edge, an isolated shape) [7], and global colour contrast, which picks up what stands out by its colour [8]. Colour weighs 60% in the model, because it is the first signal of an alarm. The computation is deterministic: the same image always gives the same values. The method is fast, inexpensive and applicable from the mock-up stage, before any user testing.

The solution

Making urgency more visible

Three changes to the interface. The elements and their layout on screen stay the same.

  1. Solid colour for life-threatening danger onlyLess serious alarms become simple outlines. What shines most is what matters most.
  2. One shape and one size per severity levelA pictogram and a format specific to each level, readable even without colour.
  3. A screen that does the sortingAs soon as several alarms are active, a count appears and stable patients recede.

  • It is 2.2 times more visible than on the original screen, in the same situation.
  • It no longer drops when other alarms appear. The 41% loss is gone.
  • Visibility now follows severity: life-threatening first, then medium severity, then technical alerts.

No information was removed from the screen; only the way alarms are displayed has changed.

For your product

What this study means for you

  • A critical interface should be tested in its most degraded scenarios. With a single alarm, the life-threatening alarm is indeed the most visible. The flaw only appears when several alarms arrive at once, which is when the risk is highest.
  • A colour code is not enough to show what is serious. Brightness, size, shape and context matter just as much.
  • This kind of flaw can be caught early, before any user testing, without mobilising clinicians or equipment.
  • For a medical device, this audit fits into formative evaluation and feeds the usability engineering file.

The principle goes beyond healthcare: control rooms, cockpits, dashboards, applications and software. Wherever a priority signal has to stand out from a busy interface, the method can apply.

Next steps

From prediction to measurement

This study predicts where the eye lands using a computational model of visual attention. It does not replace a study with participants: the next step is to verify these results with an experimental protocol, in the field.

  • Eye trackingMeasuring the clinician’s gaze (fixations, saccades) across the different versions of the interface.
  • PsychophysiologyMental workload, stress and the operator’s physiological response to the interface.
  • UX researchTask analysis, cognitive walkthroughs and interviews with clinicians.
